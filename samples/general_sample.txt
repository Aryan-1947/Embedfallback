The history of computing spans centuries, from mechanical calculators to modern silicon chips.
Early pioneers like Charles Babbage and Ada Lovelace laid theoretical groundwork long before
electronic computers existed. The mid-20th century saw vacuum tubes give way to transistors,
which in turn were miniaturized into integrated circuits. Each generation of hardware unlocked
new kinds of software, from batch-processed punch cards to interactive operating systems.

By the 1970s and 1980s, personal computers brought computing into homes and small businesses.
The rise of the internet in the 1990s connected these machines into a global network, changing
how people communicated, shopped, and worked. Mobile phones later put that same connectivity
into everyone's pocket, and cloud computing moved much of the actual processing off local
devices and into remote data centers.

Today, machine learning models trained on vast datasets can write text, generate images, and
carry on conversations. This progress didn't happen in a straight line -- it involved decades
of incremental improvements, occasional dead ends, and a few genuine breakthroughs that
reshaped what seemed possible.
